# Pre-Face

I'm a theoretical computer scientist at heart, so my opinions on the developer experience come from a very different background to most. I'm also still a student, so don't have much true industry experience, but I feel my opinion is still valid and important.

I'm also not head-strong on my opinion. I'm okay to be proved wrong. In-fact, I want to be proved wrong, as any (good) academic does

# Artifical Output

With the incredibly fast rise of generative AI in the industry, it's almost impossible to escape it. It almost feels like there's an obligiation to use some agentic editor, and that you'll "fall behind" if you don't. I do not like this. I do not like this at all.

Software Development has always been an industry pushing a need for "high output" or "high development velocity". Companies want to push products, and they want to push them fast, and software lacks many of the bottlenecks of other industries that would stop companies from doing that. This is has been a problem for well over a decade; the "slow code" movement was picking up as early as 2014 (https://lairdnelson.wordpress.com/2014/08/04/the-slow-code-movement/) as opposition to this attitude. AI only exemplifies this. With generative tools, the bar for "velocity" has been raised ever higher, and big companies love this. Why wouldn't they want to ship 200x more features? It looks great to shareholders! 

But to those (or at least me) who enjoy the pure *craft* of programming and computer science, and who find joy in the very act of programming by hand, using an agentic editor sounds like a your worst nightmare. It kills the joy and passion and effectively commodifies programming. I would love nothing more than to stay up all night implementing an interesting feature!

I think this is partially a problem with capatilism. I think most current problems AI stem more from (late stage) capatilism than anything. The technology itself is amazing, but its usages and marketing is concerning.

# To Learners

GenAI is probably the worst thing a student or learner in some field could use. There's a reason we learn how to add and multiply before being given a calculator. You lose core skills and critical thinking by learning with AI. Even if you are pro AI being used professionally, if you cannot effectively identify when an AI system is hallucinating or giving bad output, or cannot articulate the problem and task you have at hand properly, then the AI system will do no good. GIGO is a principle for any automated system, especially generative. The only person I personally see could stand to benefit from AI professionally is an expert in the field who already knows what they are doing.

# How it could be good

I don't think AI has no place at all in programming, but it needs to be done differently to how it's being done now. First and foremost, the main incentive of using it should not be velocity and profit, but adding on to that, the very paradigm of agentic editors seems flawed. Having to chat to an 'agent' to get something done is a fundamental bottleneck, and obviously I don't think getting rid of the human is the solution. There's likely much better, isolated ways of integrating AI into workflows. I see a common usage of generative systems being to produce unit tests. Perhaps there could be some system that allows you to roughly specify what a unit test should do more rigourously and have a system that uses transformers/llm technology under the hood generate tests for you.

# Conclusion

More research needs to be done. A great, great amount of research, across cognitive sciences, HCI, software engineering, and open source software.
